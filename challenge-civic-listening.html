Track 5 (Food Access & Resource Navigation) · Houston Food Bank

Where should the food bank listen first?

Partner challenge No coding needed

Read this first. This data is about places, never people. It counts how many people voted, not who they voted for. Use it only to choose where to listen. Don't use it to decide who gets food, where to put a pantry, anything about a person, or where to sign up voters or get out the vote. A low number doesn't mean people there don't care. A blank cell means the data is missing.

The problem

The Houston Food Bank wants to hear from the communities it serves, through focus groups and outreach conversations across its 10 counties: Austin, Brazoria, Chambers, Fort Bend, Harris, Liberty, San Jacinto, Trinity, Walker and Waller. It can't be everywhere at once. Voter turnout is one rough sign of which communities are heard less often in public life, so places with lower turnout may be good places to listen first. Build a tool that helps it decide where to listen first, and explain each choice in plain language with the numbers behind it.

Who it's for

Food bank staff who plan focus groups and outreach conversations across the 10 counties.

What to build

The minimum is a target, not a rule. Judges score every team with the same five criteria. All of it works in a spreadsheet, so no coding is needed.

Build at minimum

  • Your own score. Pick up to 3 columns: voter turnout (P1), civic organizations, and one more if you want, such as registered voters. Decide how much each one counts (its weight), and say why. We suggest P1 counts for at least half, and civic organizations for a quarter or less. Leave the five neighborhood conditions out of your score. They are background only.
  • Keep the not-ranked rows grey, and highlight every row that has a caution flag.
  • A shortlist of 8 places across at least 4 counties. Include both ends: places to "listen first" (lower measured participation) and places to "work with" (higher measured participation). Include both city and rural places.
  • A 2 to 3 line reason for each place, citing its numbers.
  • The AI part: an AI assistant drafts each reason from that row's numbers. Your team checks every number against the table. One slide shows a correction you made, with the ZIP code and place name removed.
  • A one-page "how to read this" for food bank staff.

Stretch goals

  • Does your shortlist hold up with a second, different weighting? Show what changes.
  • Group ZIP areas into community types using only P1 and civic organizations. Give them neutral labels ("Type A", "Type B") and show the numbers behind each type. Don't use the neighborhood conditions to make groups.
  • An English and Spanish focus-group guide, adapted from a fixed, neutral template. AI may change only the logistics, the reading level and the language. A person on your team reviews every guide. The template will be posted on this page before the event.

Team roles that work

Five roles: data, score sheet, map, pitch, and one person for responsible use and the focus-group guide.

Start here

  • No code? Open zcta_civic.xlsx in Google Sheets or Excel. Read the read_me sheet first. The not-ranked rows are already grey and sorted to the bottom.
  • Python? Row 1 of each CSV is a note on how the data may be used, so skip it, and keep ZIP codes as text: pd.read_csv(path, skiprows=1, dtype={"ZCTA": str, "county_main": str}). See open a Colab notebook and load a CSV.
  • Start with the county table to see the whole region, then look at ZIP areas inside one county.

Good to know

Rules for this challenge
  • Areas, never people. Don't look up, match or name any person, voter or household.
  • Nonpartisan. Use only the turnout counts in this table. Don't add party, candidate, primary or vote-choice data, and don't download candidate files yourself.
  • Don't add outside contact lists, such as neighborhood association lists. They include people's names and emails.
  • Your shortlist is for listening: focus groups and outreach conversations. Don't use it to choose where to run voter registration or get-out-the-vote.
  • No prompt or AI output may guess race, ethnicity, citizenship or attitudes from area numbers.
  • Use the five neighborhood conditions as background only. Don't put them in your score or use them to group areas.
  • Describe areas, not people. Don't call any area "disengaged", a "civic desert" or "underperforming".
  • On your slides and in your live demo, use county views only. Your ZIP-level shortlist goes in your team folder for the food bank, where judges can open it. Presentations are recorded.
What P1 means
  • P1 is the share of adult citizens who voted, averaged over the 2022 and 2024 November general elections. Adults held in jails or prisons are left out of the count.
  • P1_rank runs from 0 to 100 among ranked ZIP areas. 100 means the highest share voted.
  • The number of adult citizens comes from a survey, so it's an estimate. Each rank has a likely low and high (rank_p05 and rank_p95), usually about 15 points apart. If two areas' ranges overlap, treat them as about the same.
  • Voting districts and ZIP areas have different borders. HAI split each district's votes across ZIP areas based on where adults live. County totals are exact. ZIP numbers are estimates, least reliable in rural areas and in rows with the SPLIT caution flag.
  • P1 is above 1 in 5 rows. All 5 are not ranked: their populations are too small for the survey count of adults to be trusted. GROWTH is a different flag. It marks areas where 2024 registration is well above the 2020 adult population.
Why 65 ZIP areas aren't ranked
  • An area isn't ranked when its number would mislead. That happens when:
    • less than half its land is in the 10 counties;
    • many of its people live outside the 10 counties;
    • fewer than 1,500 people live there;
    • there's no usable count of adult citizens; or
    • many people live in group housing, such as dorms, nursing homes, jails or prisons.
    The not_ranked_reason column says which one.
  • A not-ranked area doesn't have low turnout. The data just can't describe it well. Together these areas hold about 4 percent of the region's registered voters.
  • In Walker, Liberty and San Jacinto counties most ZIP areas are too small or cross county lines, so a ZIP ranking doesn't describe most of the county. Work at county level there.
  • Rows with a caution flag are ranked but need care. The read_me sheet explains each flag.
Civic organizations and neighborhood conditions
  • civic_orgs_n counts IRS-registered community, neighborhood, service club and citizen participation groups. Homeowner and property owner associations are counted separately in hoa_like_n.
  • Groups are counted at their IRS mailing address, which may not be where they meet. Groups with PO box ZIP codes are lost. Churches, mutual aid groups, tenant groups and informal groups often don't file with the IRS, so they aren't counted.
  • There is no public neighborhood association list for the region outside the City of Houston.
  • 77 ZIP areas show 0 civic organizations. That means none were found in this file, not that none exist.
  • The five neighborhood conditions (same house as a year ago, owner-occupied homes, high school diploma, households that aren't limited-English, broadband) are context only. They aren't in P1.

How it's judged

Same five criteria as every track. A strong demo shows your score and why you weighted it that way, a county view of where to listen first and places to work with, the AI-drafted reasons with one correction your team caught (ZIP code removed), and an honest word on where the data is weak.
