Track 5 (Food Access & Resource Navigation)

Where will food be needed next?

Some coding helps

The problem

Food banks in the Houston area send food to many partner agencies and pantries. When a hurricane, a freeze or a wave of job losses hits, need can jump fast, and not always in the usual places. Planners have to decide where to send extra food and mobile pantries before the full numbers come in. Build an early-warning tool that shows where need is likely to rise next month, and test it honestly on past months.

Who it's for

Food bank staff who plan where food and mobile pantries go each month.

What to build

The minimum is a target, not a rule. Judges score every team with the same five criteria.

Build at minimum

An early-warning view for 8 counties: Harris and the 7 counties that border it (Brazoria, Chambers, Fort Bend, Galveston, Liberty, Montgomery and Waller). Every team uses the same 8, so judges can compare. It shows:

  • which counties are most likely to see need go up next month, using public signals (public numbers like weekly unemployment claims, SNAP cases and disaster declarations);
  • a test on past months. Pick a month, use only data from before it, make your prediction, then check what really happened. Do this for every month from January 2023 to August 2025;
  • how your tool compares with a simple guess: "next month will be the same as last month." Show how often you beat it. If you don't, say so. That still counts;
  • how sure the tool is: a range for each county, based on how far off it was in your test.

What you check against: the change in SNAP cases. It's the only public monthly count for each county, so it's what you can test. It isn't the same as need (see Good to know).

Stretch goals

  • Suggest where to send 10 extra mobile pantry visits across the 8 counties, and explain each one in plain language.
  • Look closely at Hurricane Beryl (July 2024). Did your tool flag the right counties in time?
  • Add the newer months (September 2025 to now) from the SNAP dashboard.
  • A "what if a hurricane hits next week" scenario.
  • Plan simple delivery routes from a public building you pick, like a county courthouse. Don't use a real food bank or pantry site.

Start here

  • No code? Download 12 monthly SNAP files, put the 8 counties in Google Sheets, and chart how cases changed month by month. Then add weekly unemployment claims for the same counties and look for jumps.
  • New to Python? See open a Colab notebook and load a CSV.

Good to know

About the data
  • Real food bank shipments, routes and pantry stock aren't public, so you test against SNAP cases instead.
  • SNAP counts people who get benefits, not people who need food. Rule changes in 2025 made fewer people eligible. So SNAP cases can go down while need goes up, because people who lose SNAP often turn to food banks. Don't treat a drop in SNAP as a drop in need. Show which counties your tool might be missing, and say this on your "AI we used and how we checked it" slide.
  • Weekly unemployment claims are the freshest signal here, about two weeks behind. In Harris County they about tripled the week after Hurricane Beryl.
  • In your test, only use data that existed before the month you predict. Treat Census and Feeding America numbers as fixed background, not as monthly signals.
  • If you make up pantry locations, trucks or stock, label them "made up" everywhere they appear.
  • Small counties have small numbers, and a jump of a few hundred cases can look huge. Show how sure your tool is.
  • The SNAP data is 44 monthly Excel files with different file names, and the dashboard months come in a different layout. Plan time to combine them.
  • Scripts: the HHSC site blocks scripts that pretend to be a web browser. Plain Python or curl works.
  • The Census API now needs a free key. Without one, use the download button on data.census.gov.
  • Use only public data. No personal information.

How it's judged

Same five criteria as every track. A strong demo shows your test on past months next to the "same as last month" guess, a range for each county (not just one number), and one county where your tool could be wrong, and why. The numbers come from the data, not from a chatbot. How judging works
